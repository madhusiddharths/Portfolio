Overview

Health Hotspot Tracker is an interactive geospatial surveillance dashboard that turns raw public-health data into a live picture of where influenza is spreading across Chicago — and, uniquely, into a personalized exposure-risk estimate computed from a user's own location history. It overlays weekly influenza-like-illness (ILI) activity on a 56-ZIP-code choropleth map, lets you click any neighborhood to drill into its metrics, and moves through the entire 2024 season — 36 weeks, 131,896 lab-tested cases — with a single slider.

The standout feature is the personal exposure model: upload a Google Timeline export and the app reconstructs where you spent time, spatially joins each location to a Chicago ZIP code, weights it by how contact-heavy the activity was, and multiplies it against that ZIP's real case rate to produce a contact-weighted probability of exposure — all processed locally, with nothing ever uploaded. I built the entire system end-to-end: the data engineering across five public datasets, the geospatial pipeline, the exposure model, and the deployed Streamlit front end.

Problem and Goal

Public influenza surveillance is published, but it's nearly unusable for an individual. The numbers live in dense CSVs and PDFs, they're reported at the city level, and they answer the question "how bad is flu season overall?" — not the question a real person actually asks: "how much risk am I personally carrying around, given where I've been?" A citywide case count tells you nothing about your own neighborhood, and nothing at all about the bus you took or the hours you spent in a crowded building.

I set out to close that gap. The goal was a tool that does two things well: (1) make official surveillance legible and explorable at the neighborhood level, so anyone can see where activity is concentrated this week; and (2) translate that population-level signal into a personal, contact-aware risk estimate grounded in the user's own movement — without ever sending their location data anywhere.

The Geospatial Centerpiece

The heart of the dashboard is a ZIP-code choropleth of Chicago. Weekly ILI activity (a 1–10 intensity level) is rendered across 56 ZIP codes with a YlOrRd color scale, so the hardest-hit neighborhoods read instantly as the deepest red. Behind it, 112 individual ZIP-boundary GeoJSON files are merged into a single FeatureCollection at load time and matched to the data on the postal-code property.

The map is fully interactive. Clicking a ZIP is wired through Plotly's selection events (on_select="rerun"), which captures the clicked region and rebinds every downstream metric and chart to that neighborhood. When the page first loads, the app doesn't pick an arbitrary default — it computes the hardest-hit ZIP for the selected week (the argmax of ILI activity) and selects it automatically, so the user lands on the most relevant area instead of a hardcoded one.

Metric Cards & Neighborhood Drill-Down

Alongside the map sits a panel of six live metric cards that recompute for whatever ZIP and week are selected, turning a dense surveillance table into an at-a-glance briefing:

  • ILI activity level for the selected ZIP (1–10).
  • Cases vs. last week — a signed week-over-week percentage change, so the trend direction is immediate.
  • Lab-tested cases citywide for the week.
  • Most-affected age group, pulled from CDC age-stratified rates.
  • Personal exposure probability for the week (the model described below).
  • Percent of tests positive, the standard surveillance positivity signal.

A week selector lets the user scrub across the whole MMWR calendar, and every card, the map, and all four charts update in lockstep — the entire dashboard is a single coherent view of one neighborhood, one week.

The Differentiator — Personal Exposure Probability

Most outbreak dashboards stop at population statistics. This one goes a step further and answers a personal question. A user can upload their own Google Timeline JSON — the location history Android phones record — and the app converts it into a contact-weighted exposure probability. The pipeline is a real geospatial-ML problem solved end to end:

  • Parsing: the Timeline export is flattened into a stream of timestamped events — activity records, Wi-Fi scans, position fixes, and place visits — each carrying a latitude/longitude where available.
  • Spatial join: using GeoPandas and Shapely, each coordinate is point-in-polygon matched against the Chicago ZIP boundaries, then forward-filled between fixes so a continuous track of "which ZIP was I in" is reconstructed from sparse GPS samples.
  • Contact weighting: consecutive events are collapsed into dwell intervals, and each interval's risk is accumulated as time × activity_weight × local_case_rate. The activity weights encode contact intensity — crowded rail transit (5×) and stationary indoor time (2×) carry far more risk per minute than running or cycling (0.5×).
  • Local case rate: local_case_rate is that ZIP's weekly lab-tested cases divided by its population (from 2021 Chicago counts), and surveillance is matched with a 2-week reporting lag to align exposure with when cases were actually detected.
  • Probability mapping: the accumulated risk is squashed into a 0–100% probability with a saturating curve, 1 − exp(−risk / 600), so risk grows quickly at first and then plateaus rather than running off to certainty.

Privacy by design. Nothing a user uploads ever leaves their session — the Timeline file is parsed in memory and discarded, no personal data is committed to the repo, and the default view runs on a small synthetic sample timeline so the demo works out of the box without anyone's real location history.

Seasonal & Demographic Analytics

Below the map, four linked charts give the population-level context behind a single week's snapshot. The seasonal curve traces every one of the 36 weeks of the 2024 season, making the 6,332-case January peak and the long decline into summer immediately legible.

A horizontal age-rate chart breaks the selected week down by demographic, drawn straight from CDC FluSurv-NET hospitalization rates. The story it tells is the expected — and clinically important — one: severity is heavily skewed toward the elderly, with the 85+ group hospitalized at roughly 25 per 100,000 at the peak.

A fourth chart resolves the week's positive tests into influenza subtypes (H1N1, H3N2, Influenza B, and unsubtyped A), so the dominant strain in circulation is visible at a glance — useful epidemiological context that mirrors how public-health teams actually read a season.

Data Engineering

The dashboard fuses five independent public datasets, each with its own schema, grain, and quirks, into one coherent view. All loading and shaping lives in a single data_processing.py module that acts as the project's single source of truth, cleanly separated from the UI:

DatasetSourceRole
Weekly flu surveillanceChicago Dept. of Public HealthCitywide cases, positivity, subtypes
ILI activity by ZIPCDPHChoropleth intensity (2,016 ZIP-weeks)
Age-stratified ratesCDC FluSurv-NETDemographic breakdown
Population countsChicago (2021)Per-ZIP denominators for case rates
ZIP boundariesPublic GeoJSONMap geometry & spatial joins

Each loader does real cleanup rather than assuming tidy input: surveillance weeks are derived from the genuine MMWR_WEEK field instead of a fabricated index; ZIP codes are filtered to Chicago's 60601–60661 range; the population file's non-numeric aggregate rows (citywide totals) are coerced out without relying on hardcoded row indices; and the 112 boundary files are merged and CRS-normalized to EPSG:4326 for correct spatial joins. The result is a dataset that actually agrees with itself across five sources reported on different calendars and geographies.

Architecture & Performance

The app is a single-page Streamlit application, migrated from an earlier multi-page Dash build — a deliberate simplification that collapsed a multi-callback Dash app into one reactive script that's far easier to reason about and deploy. The architecture keeps two concerns strictly apart: data_processing.py owns every dataset and the exposure model, while streamlit_app.py is pure presentation, so the analytics can be tested and reused independently of the UI.

Because loading five datasets and merging 112 GeoJSON files on every interaction would make the app crawl, the expensive work is wrapped in Streamlit's @st.cache_data — the full dashboard bundle is computed once and reused across reruns, and an uploaded timeline is parsed once and cached against its bytes. Every map click, week change, and slider move then re-renders instantly off the cached data. The whole thing is deployed on Streamlit Community Cloud with a Procfile fallback for any host, and ships with a pytest smoke-test suite.

Engineering Challenges Solved

  • Reconciling five mismatched public datasets: surveillance (citywide, MMWR weeks), ILI activity (per-ZIP), CDC rates (per age group), population (per ZIP, 2021), and geometry (112 files) all report on different grains. Built a single data layer that normalizes calendars, filters to the right geography, and joins them into one consistent model.
  • Sparse GPS → continuous ZIP track: a Timeline export has irregular, gappy location fixes. Solved with a GeoPandas point-in-polygon spatial join plus forward-filling between fixes, reconstructing a continuous "which ZIP, when" track from sparse samples while ignoring gaps longer than four hours as "device off."
  • Turning movement into a defensible risk number: designed an interpretable exposure model that combines dwell time, activity-specific contact weights, and real local case rates, then maps the result through a saturating probability curve — every term is inspectable rather than a black box.
  • Dash → Streamlit migration: rebuilt a multi-page, multi-callback Dash app as a single reactive Streamlit script, removing callback-graph complexity while preserving full interactivity (click-to-select, scrubbing, live upload).
  • Interaction latency: eliminated per-click recomputation of the five-dataset bundle and GeoJSON merge with @st.cache_data, keeping every map click and slider move instant.
  • Privacy with a working demo: kept all real location data out of the repo and processed uploads only in memory, while shipping a synthetic sample timeline so the exposure feature still works for anyone on first load.
