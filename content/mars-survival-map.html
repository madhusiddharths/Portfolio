Overview

Martian Map is a 3D map of Mars that plans the safest trip across the surface for a crew with a rover. You turn the planet like Google Earth, over NASA CTX imagery at 5 m per pixel draped on 200 m terrain, and switch between five data layers that show what can hurt or help a crew: elevation and slope, ground firmness, surface temperature, buried water ice and radiation dose.

Pick a start and a destination up to 500 km apart and the planner draws two trips, the safest and the quickest. The crew drives as far as the rover can go and walks the rest. Each trip lists its time, distance, climb, coldest ground, radiation dose and every hazard on the way, and the planner says so when no route can avoid a hazard. An immersive view then puts you 5 m above the ground at any spot you click.

Problem & Goal

The data a Mars crew would need already exists, but it sits in separate archives, in different formats and at different resolutions: a 15 GB elevation file at USGS, thermal maps in the Planetary Data System, ice maps from the SWIM project, one radiation measurement in a Science paper. None of it answers the question a crew would ask: can I get from here to there, and what will the ground do to me on the way?

The goal was one map that puts those datasets on the same grid, shows each as a layer a person can read, and turns them into a route with the hazards named. We also wanted every number to be traceable, so each layer states where its data comes from and where it stops being reliable.

The Base Map

The viewer is a single page on the ArcGIS Maps SDK for JavaScript, modelled on Caltech's Murray Lab CTX viewer. The imagery is the global CTX mosaic from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. The full mosaic is 5.6 TB, so the viewer streams it. The terrain is the NASA/USGS blend of MOLA and HRSC elevation at 200 m, which the server also holds as a local file so that contours, slopes and routes read the same heights the viewer draws.

Every layer uses plain longitude and latitude on the Mars 2000 sphere and exposes the same Python call, read_...(west, south, east, north, shape). That one convention is what let four people build layers in parallel and combine them for routing on the first try. Contour lines are drawn from the elevation file at intervals from 2000 m zoomed out to 50 m zoomed in.

Hazard & Resource Layers

One data layer shows at a time, each with a legend, so their colours never mix. The contour lines change colour to stay readable over whichever layer is under them.

  • Ground firmness: thermal inertia from Mars Global Surveyor at 3 km per pixel, in six classes from loose dust that may not bear weight to bedrock.
  • Water ice: depth to the shallowest ice. Polar surface ice comes from the USGS geologic map, and buried ice within 1 m, 1 to 5 m and deeper than 5 m comes from the SWIM consistency maps.
  • Surface temperature: a thermal model of the ground driven by THEMIS thermal inertia and TES albedo, precomputed as a lookup table over 24 seasons, 24 hours, 37 latitudes, 12 thermal inertias and 5 albedos. Sliders set the season and local time, and a click draws the 24-hour curve for that spot.
  • Radiation: a modelled dose rate for Gale crater, anchored to the Curiosity RAD measurement of 0.64 mSv per day. The model thins the dose with the air column above each point and scans the horizon in 16 directions to find how much sky the terrain blocks.
  • Topography: coloured elevation with relief shading.

The temperature model was checked against Curiosity's ground-temperature sensor in Gale crater. Nightly lows agree within about 3 K; afternoon highs come out about 11 K too cold.

The Route Planner

The planner cuts the ground between A and B into 200 m cells and grades each one Clear, Caution, Danger or Extreme from slope, firmness and temperature, once for a person on foot and once for the rover. The trip is hybrid: walk to the rover, drive, park on level firm ground, walk to B. Two routes come back from the same grid:

  • Safest: the planner first finds the lowest hazard level that still connects A and B. If that level is above Clear, every possible route crosses ground that bad, and the planner reports it as unavoidable. The route then stays at or below that level and treats each metre of hazardous ground as many extra metres of flat travel.
  • Quickest: least time. Caution ground costs nothing extra; Danger and Extreme are off limits unless they are unavoidable.

Walking speed follows Tobler's hiking function. The rover slows and spends more of its range on climbs and soft ground, and cannot drive slopes over 25°. If the trip needs more than the rover's range or the walking limit, the planner shows the closest trip it found, marked not viable, with the reasons. The rover and walking numbers come from NASA's Space Exploration Vehicle concept, the Apollo rover and the EVA Walkback Test. They are planning numbers the team chose, and the range and walking limit are fields in the panel.

Immersive View

The immersive mode hides every layer except the imagery, zooms out to the whole planet and waits for a click. The camera then drops to 5 m above the ground at that spot, under a dusty Mars sky. You drag to look around and move with six on-screen buttons: 150 m/s over the ground when you are close to it, faster the higher you climb. The imagery is 5 m per pixel on 200 m terrain, so the ground at your feet is soft; horizons, crater walls and mountains are what it shows well.

Keeping the Map Fast

The server draws every map tile in Python on request, so the work was in never drawing a tile twice and never reading more of the elevation file than a tile needs. I measured each tile endpoint before and after every change with a small timing script.

  • Caching at both ends: browsers keep tiles for a day, with a version number on each tile address, and the server keeps the last 200 tiles it drew per layer.
  • Contours coloured in the browser: the server sends one colourless mask per tile, so switching the data layer does not redraw the contours.
  • Smaller terrain tiles: 16-bit integers with gzip, about 100 KB each instead of 264 KB.
  • A cloud-optimised elevation file: a tiled, compressed copy of the same heights, 3.8 GB instead of 15.2 GB. Read over a network, one contour tile went from 129 requests and 28 s to 4 requests and 0.1 s.
  • Tiles drawn ahead of time for the layers that never change: firmness, water ice and radiation.

Limitations

  • Slopes are averages over 200 m, so the map does not see short cliffs or boulders.
  • Terrain shelter from radiation is modelled inside Gale crater only, and the model leaves out solar storms.
  • Buried ice is a SWIM consistency score, not a measured amount, and is mapped between 60°S and 60°N only.
  • The temperature model ignores slopes, shadows and dust storms.
  • Speeds, ranges and hazard thresholds are planning numbers chosen by the team. None is a NASA limit.

Tech Stack

BackendPython and FastAPI: tile, legend, point-value and routing endpoints, with static hosting of the viewer.
GeospatialRasterio and NumPy for windowed reads of the rasters; SciPy for the route search (Dijkstra on a sparse graph); Pillow for tile drawing.
FrontendPlain HTML, CSS and JavaScript on the ArcGIS Maps SDK for JavaScript 4.26, with no build step.
DataMRO CTX mosaic, MOLA + HRSC elevation, TES and THEMIS thermal inertia, TES albedo, SWIM ice maps, Curiosity RAD and REMS.
DeploymentOne Docker container on Google Cloud Run, with the elevation file read from Cloud Storage.

Outcomes & Impact

  • Shipped a working 3D Mars map with five data layers, a route planner and an immersive view in one hackathon weekend, as a team of four.
  • Put global 5 m imagery, 200 m terrain and 3 km thermal and ice maps on one shared grid, so any layer can feed the route planner.
  • Planned hybrid rover-and-foot trips of up to 500 km. On the first Cloud Run deployment a 470 km route answered in 4 s.
  • Checked the temperature model against Curiosity: nightly lows within about 3 K.
  • Cut the elevation file from 15.2 GB to 3.8 GB and a networked contour tile from 28 s to 0.1 s.

Conclusion

Martian Map puts the public Mars datasets a crew would need on one map and turns them into a route with the hazards named. The hard parts were agreeing on one grid early enough for four people to build in parallel, proving when a hazard cannot be avoided, and keeping a map that is drawn in Python fast enough to feel like a globe.
