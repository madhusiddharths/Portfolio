Overview

Tool Router Ladder asks one question: how small can a model be and still drive a multi-step, tool-calling agent reliably? A 550B-parameter teacher (Nemotron 3 Ultra) runs customer-service episodes on τ²-bench retail. Its successful trajectories become training data for a ladder of Qwen2.5 students (0.5B, 1.5B and 3B) fine-tuned with LoRA. Each student is then evaluated twice: once replaying the teacher's conversations one step at a time, and once running whole episodes on its own against a simulated customer.

The gap between those two numbers is the point of the project. A student can agree with the teacher on most individual decisions and still fail almost every task, because errors compound over the 8–15 steps of a real agent episode. On top of the distilled students, the project adds preference and reinforcement post-training (DPO and step-level GRPO) and a confidence router that hands low-confidence steps back to the teacher, served with vLLM behind a LangGraph graph.

Zero spend: every teacher, simulator and judge call runs on OpenRouter's free tier (throttled to 20 requests/min and 1,000/day), and every training run fits on free Kaggle T4 GPUs or an 18 GB M3 Pro Mac.

Problem and Goal

Frontier models are good at tool calling, but routing every step of every agent conversation through a 550B model is slow and expensive. The common answer is distillation: train a small model on the big one's behaviour. What is rarely measured honestly is how the small model fails once it is in charge.

The project sets out to measure four things:

  • Step accuracy vs task success: how per-decision agreement translates into end-to-end success when mistakes compound.
  • Catalog scaling: what happens when the tool list grows from the 16 native tools to 40 and 80, padded with near-neighbour distractors from BFCL.
  • Post-training: whether DPO or GRPO on top of SFT fixes the failure modes SFT leaves behind.
  • Routing: whether the student's own token log-probs can tell when to escalate a step to the teacher.

Architecture

The pipeline has eight stages: harvest teacher trajectories, build a loss-masked SFT set, train LoRA students, post-train them, evaluate them teacher-forced and free-running, and finally put the best student behind an escalation router. Every stage writes to an append-only JSONL run log keyed by a config fingerprint, so any cell can be resumed and no number can silently mix two configurations.

Harvesting Teacher Trajectories

τ²-bench retail's 114 tasks are split by task ID into 54 train and 60 eval tasks. The teacher runs every train task, and only successful episodes are kept: 108 episodes over 46 tasks, containing 1,099 teacher decisions (703 tool calls and 396 natural-language replies). On the eval split the teacher solves 48.0% of 300 episodes (95% CI 41.9–54.1%), which is the ceiling every student is measured against.

The harvest loop had to survive a free-tier API. throttle.py paces requests and resumes after the daily cap; judge.py swaps τ²'s hard-coded GPT-4.1 judge for an OpenRouter model (40 of the 114 tasks need a natural-language judge) and strips the markdown fences it wraps around JSON. invariants.py aborts the run if the teacher isn't pinned, the user simulator isn't frozen, the judge is the same model as the teacher, or the train and eval splits overlap.

The SFT converter serializes each episode in Qwen2.5's native tool-call format (ChatML with <tool_call> and <tool_response> blocks) and masks the loss to the teacher's own turns. The serializer's hash is pinned in configs/base.yaml; changing a single character of the prompt format aborts every run until the pin is updated.

Training the Students

Each student is a LoRA adapter (r=16, α=32, lr 1e-4, 3 epochs) on Qwen2.5-Instruct, trained on Kaggle T4s with a 12,288-token context, chosen after measuring the real training episodes (p50 7,972 tokens, max 12,176). Losses: 0.5B 0.886 → 0.539, 1.5B 0.734 → 0.442, and 3B down to 0.387 sharded over two T4s.

Making 12k-token training fit on a T4: Qwen2.5 uses grouped-query attention. Transformers passes the unexpanded KV heads to SDPA with enable_gqa=True, which no fused kernel accepts on the T4's sm75 architecture, so attention silently fell back to the math backend and materialised a [1, 14, 12176, 12176] fp16 score matrix: 7.7 GiB on a 14.6 GiB card. Forcing the KV expansion below sm80 costs about 22 MB and brings the peak down to 5.4 GiB at 0.5B and 8.4 GiB at 1.5B, with identical arithmetic.

Student inference later moved to the Mac with MLX (merged adapters, fp16, greedy decoding, prefix-cache reuse). A calibration run against Kaggle vLLM matched to within run-to-run noise: 73.4% vs 73.3% step agreement, with 93.7% of completions byte-identical.

Teacher-Forced Evaluation: The Catalog Cliff

Forced evaluation replays 142 successful teacher episodes (1,416 steps) and asks the student for the next decision at every step, with the teacher's history as context. Fine-tuning is worth +50 points or more over zero-shot at every size, but every adapter was trained on the 16-tool catalog, and accuracy falls as distractor tools are added.

Model 16 tools 40 tools 80 tools
Qwen2.5-3B + LoRA78.5%75.8%57.5%
Qwen2.5-1.5B + LoRA73.3%54.4%38.1%
Qwen2.5-0.5B + LoRA66.8%28.6%32.7%
Qwen2.5-3B zero-shot26.9%27.4%23.3%
Qwen2.5-1.5B zero-shot18.9%8.3%7.7%

The failure is specific. At 1.5B, decision-type accuracy stays around 77% at every catalog size, while tool-name accuracy falls from 76.8% to 57.0% to 41.3%. The model still knows when to call a tool; it loses which one, and near-neighbour confusion grows from 43% to 66% of wrong tool-call steps. Scaling to 3B mostly removes the 40-tool cliff (−2.7 points instead of −18.9) and halves the drop at 80 tools.

A five-bucket failure taxonomy (validated against 30 hand labels at 100% agreement) shows SFT's dominant error is call_instead_of_reply: the fine-tuned students over-call tools when the teacher would have answered the customer.

Free-Running Evaluation: Where Reliability Breaks

Free-running evaluation puts the student in charge of whole episodes inside τ²'s orchestrator, against an LLM user simulator and judge: 60 eval tasks × 5 seeds = 300 episodes per cell, reported with 95% CIs across seeds. To make this affordable, students are served behind a local vLLM OpenAI-compatible server and run N episodes concurrently, about 10× cheaper in GPU time per episode than the serial path.

Agent 16 tools 40 tools 80 tools
Teacher (550B)48.0%55.3%54.7%
Qwen2.5-1.5B + LoRA7.7% (5.8–9.5)3.0% (1.3–4.7)2.7% (1.5–3.8)
Qwen2.5-0.5B + LoRA1.3% (−0.4–3.1)3.7% (2.7–4.6)3.0% (2.1–3.9)
Qwen2.5-3B + LoRA8.7%*—2.7%

The headline result: 3B's large forced-eval gain (57.5% vs 38.1% at 80 tools) does not show up in task success, which is 2.7% for both. Reading the trajectories explains why. 3B is far more tool-happy, making 8.4 tool calls per episode with 5.2 of them to distractors, and loops through "tool not found" errors and guessed lookups until τ²'s error limit ends the episode. Smaller students fail mostly by looping on the same lookup until they hit the step limit. Across all 1,800 Stage A episodes there were zero parse errors and zero context overflows: the students produce well-formed calls, just not the right sequence of them.

* The 3B 16-tool cell stopped at 263/300 episodes when the simulator model lost its provider; it is reported as a partial result and never topped up with a different simulator.

Beyond SFT: DPO vs GRPO

Version 2 attacks SFT's failure modes with post-training, with DPO and GRPO trained as parallel branches from the same SFT checkpoint.

DPO on the student's own mistakes. Greedy completions over all 1,099 train states produced 328 preference pairs for the 1.5B student, each checked against a replayed τ²-bench database so that execution-equivalent calls are never labelled "wrong". The custom DPO loop (TRL's full-logit approach does not fit 12k tokens on a T4) reuses the SFT masked-position loss with a factorised backward pass, tested equal to the joint-loss gradient. It failed its kill gate: plain DPO collapsed to never calling a tool, and an NLL-anchored variant scored below SFT (1.5B 62.5% vs 73.3%).

Step-level GRPO worked. The reward scores each step on whether the tool exists, the name matches, the arguments fit the schema, and the call is execution-equivalent. Trained only on the 16-tool catalog, it improved the 1.5B student at every catalog size, with paired 95% CIs that exclude zero:

1.5B catalog Step agreement (SFT → GRPO) Reply steps answered as replies
16 tools73.4% → 76.9%54.4% → 63.0%
40 tools54.4% → 57.9%59.3% → 69.5%
80 tools38.1% → 40.3%60.9% → 67.1%

GRPO directly reduces SFT's over-calling habit without collapsing the other way: call steps are still called 87–90% of the time, with zero parse errors. The 0.5B student shows the same pattern (28.6% → 33.5% at 40 tools).

The Confidence Router

The router is a LangGraph graph: the student proposes each step, and if its confidence is missing or below a threshold, the step is escalated to the teacher. Confidence comes from token log-probs recorded at generation time (top-20 per token), split into a call-vs-reply score and a tool-name-span score.

An offline simulation over the 1.5B student's 80-tool run found that tool-name confidence alone is the better signal: at a matched 50% escalation rate it reaches 85.5% accuracy after escalation (AUROC 0.797) versus 82.8% for the combined signal (AUROC 0.760). Decision confidence catches calls-instead-of-replies well (AUROC 0.95) but premature replies are made confidently, so it adds noise.

The served path runs vLLM and the router with docker compose up, exposing POST /route. A load test on a single T4 with ~18k-token prompts gives a p50 of 10.1 s at concurrency 1 and throughput saturating around 0.13 decisions/s by concurrency 8: an honest latency floor dominated by prefill, and the input for the deployment stage rather than a deployable configuration.

Engineering for Honest Numbers

  • Config fingerprints: every result row is keyed by a hash of the config that produced it; optional v2 sections drop out of the fingerprint when unset, and a golden test pins the v1 values.
  • Simulator epochs: when the user-simulator model was retired mid-project, results moved to a separately labelled epoch with a re-run teacher baseline (60.3% at 80 tools). The stats code refuses any file that mixes simulators.
  • Episode-level CIs: steps inside one episode share a task and a conversation prefix, so intervals are taken across episodes; a step-level CI would be about three times too narrow.
  • No silent skips: forced evaluation refuses a completions file with any missing step, since skipping would inflate agreement on exactly the hard cases.
  • CI: GitHub Actions runs ruff, pyright, 353 pytest tests and smoke runs, with a test that keeps torch out of the core modules so CI stays light.

Key Findings

  • Step accuracy is not task success. A 1.5B student that agrees with the teacher on 73% of steps completes 7.7% of tasks, against the teacher's 48%.
  • Small models know when to act, not what to call. Adding distractor tools leaves decision accuracy flat and destroys tool-name accuracy.
  • Bigger students don't automatically help. 3B's forced-eval gains vanished in free running because it explores distractors more aggressively.
  • GRPO beats DPO here. Step-level GRPO with execution-checked rewards improved every catalog; DPO collapsed or regressed.
  • Route on tool-name confidence. It is the stronger escalation signal (AUROC 0.797).

Status and Next Steps

The project is in active development. Version 1 (SFT ladder, forced and free-running evaluation, router) is complete and being written up; GRPO for the 3B student is training on Kaggle. Next are the headline free-running comparison of SFT vs GRPO at 3B under the current simulator, the escalation sweep (Stage D) across four thresholds, and a 7B rung once GPU budget allows.
